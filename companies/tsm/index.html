TSM 20-F Redline Analysis

Year-over-year textual analysis of Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company's 20-F filings reveals the arc from pure foundry to geopolitical linchpin -- export controls, geographic expansion, AI demand, and market redefinition tracked through seven years of SEC disclosure.
Source: SEC EDGAR (CIK 0001046179) | 20-F Filings, FY2018-2025 | Analysis: May 2026; FY2023-FY2025 transitions re-analyzed October 2026
FY2018 → FY2019

Export Controls Emerge

End-market categories rewritten. Export controls added to customer risk. #2 customer crosses 10% threshold. GlobalFoundries litigation.

FY2019 → FY2020

Huawei & Arizona

Huawei shipments halted September 2020. Arizona fab first disclosed. Droughts added to disaster risks. China blocking statute.

FY2020 → FY2021

Global Expansion

"Military" added to geopolitical heading. Japan (JASM) disclosed. Global subsidy race language. "Large capacity" becomes "scale and capacity."

FY2021 → FY2022

CHIPS Act & Controls

CHIPS Act named. October 2022 export controls. Global operations complexity risk factor added. Nuclear-free language dropped. Nanjing demoted behind Arizona.

FY2022 → FY2023

AI Surge

AI first appears in risk factors. "Highly competitive" becomes "fierce." HPC listed before Smartphone. AI in cybersecurity risk. Supply chain diversification as competitive factor.

FY2023 → FY2024

"Largest" Dropped, Subsidies Signed

Both "largest dedicated foundry" claims removed; share metric switched to "Foundry 2.0." Antitrust risk names TSMC itself. January 2025 16nm global licensing rule; October 2024 chip-diversion notice. CHIPS Act $6.6B + $5B with parent guarantee and new clawback risk. HPC becomes "the key growth driver."

FY2024 → FY2025

Tariff Costs & Narrower Disclosure

Tariffs "affected our cost of operations in the U.S."; Section 232 25% chip tariff; U.S.-Taiwan deal. March 2025 U.S. expansion plan deleted. Capex US$40.9B, 2026 guide US$52-56B, with a new "if the market demand does not materialize" hedge. Share metric and customer roster removed; #2 customer 17%.

FY2018 → FY2019: Export Controls Emerge

Filed April 2020 | Revenue: NT$1,031.5B → NT$1,069.9B (+4%)

Business & Risk Factors

▶End-market categories rewritten from product types to application typesChanged
FY2018: "derived from customers who use our services in communication products, computing products, consumer electronics products and industrial/standard products"
FY2019: "who use our products in smartphones, high performance computing, IoT, automotive electronics, and digital consumer electronics"
▶Export control added to customer concentration riskNew
"regulatory restrictions such as export control directed at our major customers could impact our ability to supply products to those customers, reduce those customers demand for our products and services and impact their business operations"
▶#2 customer crosses 10% revenue threshold at 14%Signal
Second-largest customer share rose to 14% of net revenue, crossing the named-disclosure threshold for the first time.
▶GlobalFoundries patent infringement litigation disclosedNew
"assertions and litigations initiated by semiconductor companies seeking to disrupt our business such as the patent infringement lawsuits in August 2019 filed by GlobalFoundries (GF)"

To comply with relevant laws and regulations, we have discontinued shipment of products to Huawei since September 15, 2020."
▶China blocking statute disclosed as counter-measureNew

Capacity & Operations

▶Arizona fab first disclosed in capital expenditure plansNew
"expanding buildings/facilities for Fab 18 and a 300mm wafer fab in Arizona, United States of America"
▶"Droughts" added to disaster risk headingChanged

Filed April 2022 | Revenue: NT$1,339.3B → NT$1,587.4B (+19%)

Geopolitical & Risk Language

▶"Military" added to geopolitical risk headingChanged
FY2020: "risks associated with political, economic, financial or other conditions or developments"
FY2021: "risks associated with political, economic, financial, military or other conditions or developments in various jurisdictions"
▶Global subsidy race language introducedNew
"The governments of Europe, the United States, South Korea, and Japan also have incentive programs to incentivize developments of their domestic semiconductor industries."
▶"Distributor" dropped from inventory adjustment languageChanged
FY2020: "the timing of customer or distributor inventory adjustments"

Capacity & Competitive

▶Japan (JASM) first disclosed as subsidiaryNew
"Japan Advanced Semiconductor Manufacturing (JASM)"
Construction commenced 2022, commercial production expected 2024. TSMC owned approximately 81.0% of JASM equity as of February 28, 2022.
▶"Large capacity" upgraded to "scale and capacity"Changed
FY2020: "We believe that our large capacity, particularly for advanced technologies, is a major competitive advantage."
FY2021: "We believe that our scale and capacity, particularly for advanced technologies, is a major competitive advantage."
▶Construction risk added to expansion plansNew
With Arizona and JASM under construction simultaneously, new risk language addressed fab buildout delays, cost overruns, and hiring challenges for overseas operations.
▶HPC enters customer concentration rationaleChanged
Customer concentration explanation updated to reference the "structural shift to mobile and high performance computing" as a driver of top-customer revenue share.

FY2021 → FY2022: CHIPS Act & Controls

Filed April 2023 | Revenue: NT$1,587.4B → NT$2,263.9B (+43%)

Export Controls & Regulation

▶CHIPS Act named by full title for the first timeNew
"the Creating Helpful Incentives to Produce Semiconductors and Science Act of 2022 (the 'U.S. CHIPS Act'), which provides financial incentives to incentivize the development of U.S. semiconductor industry"
▶October 2022 export controls disclosedNew
"In October 2022, the U.S. adopted additional export controls over specified countries (including China) under the U.S. Export Administration Regulations on certain advanced computing integrated circuits, computer commodities that contain such ICs, and certain semiconductor manufacturing items"
▶Global operations complexity as standalone risk factorNew
"If we are unable to successfully manage the complexity of our global operations and deal with the challenges and risks related to our overseas expansion, our business, financial condition and results of operations could be adversely affected."
▶"Forced labor prohibition" added to compliance languageNew
Forced labor prohibition language appeared in the compliance and regulatory sections for the first time, reflecting both U.S. and EU supply chain due diligence requirements.

Capacity & Positioning

▶Nuclear-free language dropped from environmental sectionRemoved
FY2021 included "nuclear-free" in energy policy references. FY2022 removed this language entirely, coinciding with Taiwan's evolving energy policy debate as power demands for advanced fabs increased.
▶Nanjing demoted in capacity location ordering -- Arizona listed firstChanged
FY2021: "including in Taiwan, in Nanjing, China, in Arizona, U.S., and in Kumamoto, Japan"
FY2022: "including in Taiwan, in Arizona, U.S., in Nanjing, China and in Kumamoto, Japan"
▶AWS added to named customer listNew
"including, but not limited to, Advanced Micro Devices, Inc., Amazon Web Services, Inc., Broadcom Limited, Intel Corporation, MediaTek Inc., NVIDIA Corporation"
AWS appeared for the first time in FY2022, reflecting system companies designing their own silicon.

FY2022 → FY2023: AI Surge

Filed April 2024 | Revenue: NT$2,263.9B → NT$2,161.7B (-5%)

AI & Competition

▶AI appears in risk factors for the first timeNew
"the global surge in the development of artificial intelligence ('AI') has had a significant impact on customer demand for advanced semiconductor chips and the market dynamics in our industry"
▶"Highly competitive" upgraded to "fierce"Changed
FY2022: "The markets for our foundry services are highly competitive."
FY2023: "The competition in the semiconductor foundry industry is fierce."
▶Supply chain diversification added as competitive factorNew
FY2023 added "supply chain diversification and resilience" to the list of dimensions on which TSMC competes, alongside technology, capacity, quality, and customer satisfaction.
▶HPC listed before Smartphone in end-market orderingChanged
Prior filings listed "smartphones, high performance computing" in that order. FY2023 reversed to HPC first, reflecting that HPC overtook Smartphone as the largest revenue platform.

AI Risk & Cybersecurity

▶AI in cybersecurity risk -- attackers using AINew
"as AI continues to evolve, cyber-attackers could also use AI to develop malicious codes and sophisticated phishing attempts"
▶AI in internal operations acknowledged as competitive necessityNew
"We also believe that the effective use of AI in our internal operations is important to our long-term success. As the AI technologies are rapidly evolving, if we are unable to deploy new AI technologies in our internal operations as effectively as our competitors, it may hurt our competitive position."

FY2023 → FY2024: "Largest" Dropped, Subsidies Signed, Controls Go Global

Filed April 17, 2025 | Revenue: NT$2,161.7B → NT$2,894.3B (+34%) | Operating margin 42.6% → 45.7% | Net income attributable NT$851.7B → NT$1,158.4B | Capex US$30.4B → US$29.8B

Self-Description & Market Position

▶Both "largest" claims deleted: "world's largest dedicated foundry" and "largest capacity among the world's dedicated foundries" become "a strong position in manufacturing capacity"Removed
FY2023: "We believe we are currently the world's largest dedicated foundry in the semiconductor industry."
FY2023: "Since our establishment, we have possessed the largest capacity among the world's dedicated foundries."
FY2024: "Since our establishment, we have built a strong position in manufacturing capacity as a dedicated foundry."
The filing gives up both size superlatives in the same year it adds that it faces "increasing criticism on the concentration of the semiconductor industry and sometimes directly on us" (see the antitrust finding below). TSMC's position did not shrink, so the deletions look like deliberate softening of dominance language, not a change in fact.
▶Share metric switched from "semiconductor excluding memory" (28%, down from 30%) to "Foundry 2.0" (34%, up from 28%)Changed
FY2023: "We produced 28 percent of the world semiconductor excluding memory output value in 2023, as compared to 30 percent in the previous year, mainly due to an inventory correction within the semiconductor industry."
FY2024: "We produced 34% of the ... industry, which we define as all logic wafer manufacturing, packaging, testing, mask-making and others, an increase from 28% in 2023."
The FY2023 filing had to report a share decline (30% to 28%). The new definition, which adds packaging, testing and masks to the denominator, produces a rising series. 2023 comes out at 28% under both definitions. The old metric is not carried forward, so readers cannot compare the two.
▶HPC promoted from "one of the key growth drivers" to "the key growth driver", and a new warning that AI growth may be "volatile or not sustainable"Signal
FY2023: "HPC has become one of the key growth drivers for our business."
FY2024: "HPC has become the key growth driver for our business."
FY2024 (new, customer risk factor): "These shifting business models could lead to significant variations in our sales if the growth of their products and services, particularly in the AI sector, is volatile or not sustainable."
The end-market risk factor also renames the segment "HPC (including AI applications)". The business section names HPC as the single growth engine, while the risk factors add the filing's first warning that AI demand may not last. HPC was 51% of revenue (NT$1,476.9B, +58%).
▶Antitrust risk rewritten to name TSMC itself as a target of "increasing criticism" over concentrationNew
FY2024: "With our success in the foundry business and the increasing criticism on the concentration of the semiconductor industry and sometimes directly on us, we are subject to heightened risks of antitrust investigations."
FY2023 introduced the 2017 European Commission inquiry with "For example". FY2024 replaces that with a statement that TSMC's own success raises its antitrust risk. This is the first time the filing ties antitrust exposure to its market position.

Export Controls & Trade

▶January 2025 rules: a license may be needed to ship 16nm-and-below products "to any global destination"New
FY2024: "Under the new rules, we may need to obtain an export license prior to shipping products using 16-nanometer or below process to any global destination unless specific conditions are met. As a result, shipments of certain products may be delayed or prohibited due to the license requirements and our financial results may be adversely affected."
Earlier export-control language covered China-bound items and named customers (Huawei). This rule applies to every destination and to nodes two generations behind the leading edge, so most of TSMC's advanced revenue is now potentially subject to licensing.
▶New: October 2024 notice to U.S. and Taiwan authorities of a possible chip diversion to a restricted entity, plus an admission of limited downstream visibilityNew
FY2024: "Our role in the semiconductor supply chain inherently limits our visibility and information available to us regarding the downstream use or user of final products that incorporate semiconductors manufactured by us."
FY2024: "In October 2024, we notified relevant U.S. and Taiwan authorities that one type of our customer's chip manufactured by us might have been diverted to a restricted entity or incorporated into a restricted entity's product, and since then have been cooperating with the authorities' requests for additional information and documents."
This is the first disclosed export-control incident involving TSMC's own production rather than a customer ban. The paragraph also says there is "no assurance that our business activities will not be found incompliant", which leaves open the possibility of penalties.
▶Tariffs enter the risk factors: the April 2, 2025 baseline and reciprocal tariffs, and "significant uncertainty" over chip tariffsNew
FY2024: "U.S. President Donald Trump announced on April 2, 2025 a 10% baseline tariff on imports into the United States from all countries, as well as varying reciprocal tariffs on certain trading partners, including Taiwan."
FY2024: "Semiconductors were not subject to the reciprocal tariffs announced on April 2, 2025. However, given ongoing discussions between the United States and its trading partners, there remains significant uncertainty about whether the United States may further change the scope and level of tariffs it imposes, including any potential tariffs it may impose on semiconductor imports."
The filing went in two weeks after the announcement and names Taiwan. Its risk is demand: tariffs on chip-containing products "may, in turn, lead to decreased demand for our products". It does not yet discuss TSMC's own cost of operating in the U.S. (that arrives in FY2025).
▶Nanjing: VEU status moves from "applying for" to obtained, and Nanjing drops out of the list of places TSMC is adding capacityChanged
FY2023: "This general authorization has been renewed and extended to be effective until May 31, 2024. We are also applying for a Validated End-User ... authorization for our Nanjing fab"
FY2024: "which is a permanent authorization that allows our fab in Nanjing to receive exports of eligible items from the U.S. without separate licenses"
FY2023: "we have continued to add capacity to meet market needs for our products and services, including in Taiwan, in Arizona, U.S., in Nanjing, China, in Kumamoto, Japan and in Dresden, Germany."
FY2024: "we have continued to add capacity to meet market needs for our products and services, including in Taiwan, in Arizona, U.S., in Kumamoto, Japan and in Dresden, Germany."
Nanjing's supply of U.S. tools is now covered by a "permanent" authorization, yet the filing stops listing it as a growth site. Nanjing appears to have become maintain-only. FY2025 then reports that this "permanent" VEU expired.

Subsidies & Overseas Expansion

▶CHIPS Act signed: up to US$6.6B grants + US$5B loans, backed by an "irrevocable, absolute and unconditional" parent guarantee; EUR5B German aidNew
FY2024: "Under this agreement, the DOC agrees to award TSMC Arizona up to US$6.6 billion in direct funding related to TSMC Arizona's semiconductor manufacturing facilities in Phoenix, Arizona"
FY2024: "the Company entered into certain guarantee agreements, under which the Company agrees to provide an irrevocable, absolute and unconditional guarantee to the DOC regarding any financial obligations owed by TSMC Arizona to the DOC. The awards under the Direct Funding Agreement are subject to various conditions, compliance requirements, program requirements and project milestone requirements."
The headline amounts were public in November 2024. What the filing adds is that the Taiwan parent guarantees its Arizona subsidiary's obligations to the U.S. government, and that the awards depend on milestones. ESMC separately signed for "up to EUR5 billion state aid under the European Chips Act".
▶Subsidy risk rewritten: clawbacks, forfeiture and a grantor that may "be unable to provide the funding"; competition heading adds "equal access to governmental financial incentives"Changed
FY2023: "the grantors could seek to recover any funds provided to us, or cancel, reduce or deny our requested subsidies or grants in the future."
FY2024: "Noncompliance with the terms and conditions of the grants that we may receive could result in a delay or forfeiture of all or a portion of any future amounts to be received, as well as obligate us to repay all or a portion of amounts already received pursuant to the grants. Even if we satisfy the conditions and requirements for the funding disbursement, it is possible that the grantor may delay the disbursement or be unable to provide the funding."
FY2024: "if we are unable to compete effectively in the highly competitive foundry segment of the semiconductor industry, including through equal access to governmental financial incentives, especially those available to our competitors, we may lose customers"
The new risk covers a grantor that does not pay even when TSMC complies, which was a live political question in early 2025. The "equal access" clause frames subsidies to rivals as a competitive threat. The overseas-expansion list likewise adds "non-receipt, delay and potential claw backs of government subsidies".
▶Arizona: "three" fabs becomes "multiple"; Fab 21 in volume production by end-2024 (target had been 2025); March 2025 plan for three more fabs, two packaging plants and an R&D centerNew
FY2023: "TSMC Arizona plans to build and operate three advanced semiconductor manufacturing facilities in Phoenix, Arizona. Construction on the first facility commenced in April 2021 and commercial production is targeted in 2025."
FY2024: "TSMC Arizona plans to build and operate multiple advanced semiconductor manufacturing facilities in Phoenix, Arizona. Construction on the first facility commenced in April 2021 and volume production commenced by the end of 2024."
FY2024: "In March 2025, we further announced our intention to build three additional semiconductor manufacturing facilities, two advanced packaging facilities and a R&D center in the United States."
The site count is left open-ended, the first fab beat its target, and the March 2025 U.S. commitment appears as an "intention". Track the "intention" sentence: the FY2025 filing deletes it.

Pricing, Costs & Disclosure

▶Pricing rationale extended: price now also funds "healthy financial conditions to expand capacity"Changed
FY2023: "Our pricing enables us to continue to invest significantly in research and development to deliver ever-improving products to our customers."
FY2024: "Our pricing enables us to continue to invest significantly in research and development to deliver ever-improving products to our customers, and sustain healthy financial conditions to expand capacity and support customers' growth."
Price is now justified by capacity funding as well as R&D. Read with the MD&A, which cites "higher electricity cost" in the gross-margin bridge and overseas fab start-up expenses in G&A, this sets up the argument for passing costs through to customers. The ASP sentence ("the historical trend of declining average selling prices") did not change; it already read that way in FY2023.
▶Electricity: "estimated" 25% hike becomes actual, plus another 14% in October 2024; the warning that utility shortages could curtail expansion is deletedChanged
FY2024: "effective from April 1, 2024, we became subject to a higher electricity tariff rate in Taiwan, which increased by 25% as compared to the tariff rate applicable to us in 2023. In October 2024, the electricity tariff rate further increased by 14%."
FY2023 (deleted in FY2024): "Moreover, our future capacity expansions in Taiwan and elsewhere could be curtailed by utility shortages."
Power became a named margin headwind: the MD&A says the 2024 gross margin gain was "partially offset by 3-nanometer ramp-up and higher electricity cost". In the same year the filing deleted the warning that power or water shortages could cap expansion. FY2025 restores a version of it.
▶Carbon fees move from "could be required" to "we will start to pay" from 2026Changed
FY2023: "We could be required to pay any incurred carbon fees since our emission levels exceed applicable thresholds pursuant to the current regulatory requirements, which will result in increased operating costs for us and affect us financially to a certain extent."
FY2024: "As our emissions from each fab in Taiwan exceed the current regulatory threshold, we will start to pay carbon fees from 2026 (for the 2025 fees), which will result in increased manufacturing costs for us."
"Could" becomes "will" and the cost now has a start date, a commitment-strength change in the cost-headwind direction. The FY2023 line that the rate "has yet to be determined" is gone.
▶Raw-wafer supplier names and their 96% share of supply removedRemoved
FY2023: "The principal suppliers for our wafers are Formosa SUMCO Technology Corporation of Taiwan, GlobalWafers of Taiwan, Shin-Etsu Handotai of Japan, Siltronic AG of Germany, Soitec Microelectronics of Singapore, and SUMCO Corporation of Japan. Together they supplied approximately 96.0%, 95.0% and 96.2% of our total wafer needs in 2021, 2022 and 2023, respectively."
FY2024: "In pursuit of high quality raw wafers, the majority of our raw wafers are supplied by a limited number of suppliers located in Taiwan, Japan, Germany, and Singapore."
A named, quantified supplier concentration becomes an unquantified "majority". This is the same kind of cut as the customer roster removed in FY2025: counterparties and percentages are being taken out.
▶Marlin (Longitude Licensing) ITC patent case filed February 2025 replaces the settled Daedalus matterNew
FY2024: "In February 2025, Longitude Licensing Ltd. and Marlin Semiconductor Limited (collectively, ... filed complaints with the U.S. International Trade Commission ... and the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Texas alleging that TSMC and its customers infringe five U.S. patents."
An ITC case can lead to an exclusion order on imports, and this complaint also names TSMC's customers. It is the only material litigation disclosed. FY2025 adds only that the Texas case was stayed.

FY2024 → FY2025: Tariffs Hit U.S. Costs, the U.S. Plan Goes Quiet, Disclosure Narrows

Filed April 16, 2026 | Revenue: NT$2,894.3B → NT$3,809.1B (+31.6%; US$121.4B) | Gross margin 56.1% → 59.9% | Operating margin 45.7% → 50.8% | Net income attributable NT$1,158.4B → NT$1,697.6B (+46.5%) | Capex US$29.8B → US$40.9B (FY2025 figures from the 20-F text; not yet in SEC XBRL)

Tariffs & Export Controls

▶Reciprocal tariffs exempted chips but "nonetheless affected our cost of operations in the U.S."; struck down February 2026, then replaced by a Section 122 tariff and Section 301 probes that name TaiwanNew
FY2024: "Semiconductors were not subject to the reciprocal tariffs announced on April 2, 2025."
FY2025: "Collection of the reciprocal tariffs was delayed until August 2025, and while they excluded semiconductors, semiconductor manufacturing equipment and their respective derivative products (collectively ... these tariffs nonetheless affected our cost of operations in the U.S. These tariffs were declared unlawful by a February 2026 U.S. Supreme Court ruling."
FY2025: "Following the Supreme Court ruling, the U.S. imposed a replacement tariff under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 and in March 2026, the U.S. Trade Representative, using authority in Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, initiated two investigations relating to alleged manufacturing overcapacity and failures to take action on forced labor, targeting various economies, including Taiwan"
FY2024's tariff risk was about customers' demand. FY2025 is the first filing to say tariffs raised TSMC's own costs in Arizona, even with chips exempt. The Supreme Court ruling did not end the exposure: a replacement tariff followed and two new investigations name Taiwan.
▶Section 232 ends in a 25% tariff on certain advanced computing chips; the January 2026 U.S.-Taiwan deal caps reciprocal rates at 15% and promises "preferential" 232 treatment, but the details "are still unclear"New
FY2025: "the U.S. issues a proclamation imposing a 25% ad valorem tariff on the import of certain advanced computing chips and derivative products meeting specified technical performance thresholds, unless such products are imported for designated use in the U.S."
FY2025: "The agreement also grants preferential Section 232 treatment for Taiwanese semiconductor producers investing in U.S. manufacturing capacity. However, details regarding the impacts of the U.S. Supreme Court decision striking down the IEEPA reciprocal tariffs as well as the preferential treatment with respect to Section 232 tariffs, including any offsets, are still unclear."
FY2025: "Any tariffs imposed on imports of semiconductor manufacturing materials and equipment into the U.S. may result in increased costs for our manufacturing operations in the U.S. and reduce our profitability."
The preferential treatment depends on "investing in U.S. manufacturing capacity", which ties tariff relief to the Arizona build-out. That gives weight to the deletion of the March 2025 U.S. expansion plan below. The last sentence is a new risk: tariffs on the tools and materials TSMC itself imports for its U.S. fabs.
▶Export-control compliance: "business partners" becomes "customers" as possible diverters, and foundries "may not be able to verify" what they makeChanged
FY2024: "This constraint impedes our ability to fully ensure that semiconductors manufactured by us will not be diverted to unintended end use or end-user, including potentially by our business partners, or by third parties with an intent of circumvention."
FY2025: "This constraint impedes our ability to fully ensure such semiconductors will not be diverted to unintended end use or end-user, including potentially by our customers, or by third parties. In addition, export controls over certain semiconductors often depend on the products' specific features or performance characteristics that foundries may not be able to verify independently."
TSMC now names its own customers as possible sources of diversion. It also argues that a foundry cannot independently verify whether a design meets export-control thresholds, which positions it to put the compliance burden on customers if the October 2024 diversion inquiry (still disclosed as ongoing cooperation) leads to penalties.
▶Nanjing's "permanent" VEU expired December 2025; the fab now runs on an annual export license that may not be renewed in timeChanged
FY2024: "which is a permanent authorization that allows our fab in Nanjing to receive exports of eligible items from the U.S. without separate licenses"
FY2025: "the U.S. Department of Commerce has granted TSMC Nanjing Company Limited (... an annual export license that allows U.S. export-controlled items to be supplied to TSMC Nanjing, which ensures uninterrupted fab operations and product deliveries. However, there is no assurance that this annual export license will not be terminated, or we can timely get the license renewed in the future."
A year after being called "permanent", the authorization expired. Nanjing now depends on a yearly license, which adds renewal risk on top of the risk of termination.
▶Huawei disappears from the filing; the January 2025 16nm rule is narrowed from "any global destination" to "specified destinations"Removed
FY2024 (deleted): "To comply with relevant laws and regulations, we have discontinued shipment of products to Huawei since September 2020."
FY2024: "we may need to obtain an export license prior to shipping products using 16-nanometer or below process to any global destination unless specific conditions are met"
FY2025: "nanometer or below process to specified destinations unless specific conditions are met"
This is the first filing since FY2020 with no reference to Huawei. The 16nm licensing requirement is narrowed to specified destinations, consistent with the U.S. stepping back from the worldwide licensing framework issued in January 2025 (an inference; the filing does not say why).

U.S. Build-Out, Capex & Capacity

▶March 2025 U.S. plan deleted: no mention of "three additional" fabs, "two advanced packaging facilities" or an R&D center; Arizona text now ends at fab three plus "an additional parcel of land"Removed
FY2024: "In March 2025, we further announced our intention to build three additional semiconductor manufacturing facilities, two advanced packaging facilities and a R&D center in the United States."
FY2025: "Our first facility successfully entered high volume production at the end of 2024. Construction of our second facility is ongoing, and the construction of our third facility commenced in 2025. Furthermore, we acquired an additional parcel of land in 2026 to support our current expansion plans and provide more flexibility in response to strong customer demand."
The FY2025 filing does not mention the March 2025 commitment anywhere, although the trade deal ties Section 232 relief to U.S. investment. What the filing now commits to is three fabs, one in production, plus land. It does not say whether the deletion means a scaled-back plan or more cautious drafting.
▶Capex US$29.8B to US$40.9B, guided to US$52-56B for 2026, while the capacity risk factor gains a new hedge: "if the market demand does not materialize"Signal
FY2025: "Our capital expenditures in 2026 are expected to be between US$52 billion and US$56 billion, which, depending on market conditions, may be adjusted later."
FY2024: "If we do not increase our net revenue accordingly, our financial performance may be adversely affected by these increased costs."
FY2025: "If the market demand does not materialize, and/or we do not increase our net revenue accordingly, our financial performance may be adversely affected by these increased costs."
The FY2025 capex guide (US$38-42B a year earlier) was met at US$40.9B (NT$1,272.4B), and unrecorded capital purchase obligations rose from NT$1,144.1B to NT$1,534.6B. Alongside the step-up, the risk factor adds for the first time that the demand may not arrive. This is the most explicit overbuild caveat in the three filings reviewed here.
▶Customer capacity prepayments fall a third: temporary receipts NT$291.1B to NT$189.9B in a year revenue rose 32%Signal
FY2025: "some of our customers have entered into agreements with us to pay temporary receipts in order to retain specified capacity at our fabs"
The contractual-obligations table shows advance temporary receipts from customers of NT$278.3B (end-2023), NT$291.1B (end-2024) and NT$189.9B (end-2025). Prepayments to reserve capacity are falling while the MD&A cites "higher capacity utilization", so customers are paying less up front to hold capacity. That suggests either easier capacity access or a shift in contract terms; the filing gives no reason.
▶2nm in volume production, 16Å risk production in 2026, advanced backend fabs five to seven; reported capacity barely moves ("exceeded 17 million" vs ~17 million wafers)Changed
FY2024: "Also, the development of our 2-nanometer technology is on track, and its volume production is expected in 2025."

Themes

1. Trade Policy & Export Control Escalation Ladder

Export controls went from absent (FY2018) to generic risk (FY2019) to named customer cutoff (FY2020 Huawei) to comprehensive regime (FY2022 October rules) to global 16nm licensing requirements (FY2024). FY2025 added a Section 232 25% chip tariff (December 2025) with a tariff offset program for U.S. domestic manufacturing, IEEPA reciprocal tariffs that were reversed by the February 2026 Supreme Court ruling, two new Section 301 investigations targeting Taiwan (March 2026), and a January 2026 U.S.-Taiwan trade and investment agreement granting preferential Section 232 treatment for Taiwanese semiconductor producers investing in U.S. capacity. The Nanjing VEU expired December 2025, replaced with an annual export license. The 16nm rule scope was narrowed from "any global destination" to "specified destinations." The "Donald Trump" attribution from FY2024 was removed in favor of "the U.S. President" / "the U.S. government."

2. Geographic Diversification Under Geopolitical Pressure

Arizona appeared in FY2020, Japan (JASM) in FY2021, Germany (ESMC) in FY2022. By FY2024, TSMC had secured $6.6B + $5B from U.S. CHIPS Act and EUR5B from the European Chips Act. Nanjing was demoted from second in location ordering (FY2021) to dropped entirely (FY2024). FY2024 added a March 2025 "intention" to build three more U.S. fabs, two advanced packaging facilities and an R&D center; FY2025 deletes that sentence and describes only Fab 1 in high-volume production, Fab 2 ongoing, Fab 3 construction commenced 2025, and an additional parcel of land acquired 2026, even as the U.S.-Taiwan deal ties Section 232 relief to U.S. investment. North America revenue share rose to 75% (from 70% in FY2024); China dropped to 9% (from 11%). North America headcount nearly doubled in two years (2,668 to 5,290).

3. AI as Revenue and Risk Driver

AI was absent from all filings through FY2022. In FY2023, it appeared in risk factors, cybersecurity warnings, and internal operations language simultaneously. By FY2024, HPC was rebranded "HPC (including AI applications)" at 51% of revenue, and TSMC warned that AI-sector growth may be "volatile or not sustainable." FY2025 inserted "AI" into the megatrends list ("5G, AI and HPC"), reframed the technological-leader risk around "AI smartphones, AI PCs, and AI accelerators" (replacing "smartphones"), and HPC reached 58% of revenue while Smartphone fell to 29%. The "volatile or not sustainable" warning was retained verbatim.

4. Market Definition as Strategic Tool

TSMC's share metric changed four times: 56% of dedicated foundries (FY2018), 28% of semiconductor ex-memory (FY2023), 34% of "Foundry 2.0" (FY2024), and no metric cited (FY2025). FY2025 also dropped the named-customer roster (AMD, AWS, Broadcom, Intel, MediaTek, NVIDIA, NXP, Qualcomm, Renesas, Sony) from the Business Overview, and folded the standalone antitrust risk factor into a broader compliance heading. FY2024 had already removed the named raw-wafer suppliers and their 96% supply share, and both "largest" self-descriptions. The "increasing criticism on the concentration of the semiconductor industry and sometimes directly on us" language is retained.

5. Pricing Power Inflection

Earlier filings described "the historical and current trend of declining average selling prices"; by the FY2023 filing the phrase was already "the historical trend" (this theme previously dated the change to FY2024, which the FY2023 text contradicts). FY2024's pricing signal is different: price must now "sustain healthy financial conditions to expand capacity and support customers' growth." FY2024's competition heading also added "equal access to governmental financial incentives" -- TSMC now frames competition as subsidy-driven rather than price-driven. "Highly competitive" became "fierce" in FY2023, but the underlying competitive dynamic shifted from price to capacity access. FY2025 complicates this: competitors' "aggressive pricing initiatives" are no longer limited to "one or several technology nodes," and customers' capacity-reservation prepayments (temporary receipts) fell from NT$291.1B to NT$189.9B.

6. Customer Concentration and Platform Shift

Top-10 customer concentration rose from 68% (FY2018) to 78% (FY2025). The #2 customer crossed 10% at 14% in FY2019, dropped below 10% after FY2020, then returned above 10% in FY2023 (11%), 12% (FY2024), and 17% (FY2025). The #1 customer share moved 25% → 22% → 19% over the same period -- the gap between #1 and #2 narrowed from 14 to 2 percentage points. HPC overtook Smartphone as the largest platform in FY2022 (41% vs 40%) and reached 58% in FY2025; Smartphone fell to 29%.

7. Capex Step-Change and 2nm Production

Capex stepped from US$29.8B (FY2024) to US$40.9B (FY2025), with FY2026 guidance of US$52-56B. 2-nanometer entered volume production in 2025 at Fab 20 (Hsinchu) and Fab 22 (Kaohsiung); 16-angstrom risk production targeted for 2026. Advanced node mix (7nm and below) reached 74% of wafer revenue. The FY2026 capex bullets dropped Fab 23 (Kumamoto) from the specialty/packaging expansion list, leaving only Fab 24 (Dresden), although JASM's second site began construction in October 2025. The capacity risk factor now adds "if the market demand does not materialize" to its overbuild warning. Gross margin reached 59.9% and operating margin 50.8% -- both new highs in the filing history.
